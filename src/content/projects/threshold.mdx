## The question

What does someone need to decide before they can experience a product—not before they can configure everything? Threshold explores a goal-first onboarding path. The screens and sample choices are original concept artifacts. No real user interviews, client engagement or activation result is claimed.

The constructed baseline puts five setup groups in front of a first task: profile details, team configuration, integrations, notification preferences and advanced settings. That creates an inspectable starting point for the exercise, rather than pretending to document an existing commercial product.

## The constraint

Deferring a decision is only helpful when the product can still do something useful. A setup flow cannot simply remove required permissions or make promises about a workspace that does not exist. The proposal therefore assumes a personal draft can be created before collaborative and integration features are needed.

That assumption is central. A product with mandatory compliance checks, account verification or organization-level configuration would need a different sequence. The concept does not claim a universally correct onboarding pattern.

## Give the route names people can understand

The proposed flow has three stages: Your goal, Workspace and First task. The first screen offers a small set of starting intentions. One primary action continues the route, while Back remains explicit.

The visible route explains the relationship between the current question and what comes next. It does not imply that every stage has equal effort. The labels would need to be tested with the actual product and its audience, especially where a workspace has a domain-specific meaning.

## Defaults should remain recoverable

A contextual recommendation can help someone begin, but it becomes a trap when they cannot see or change the assumptions behind it. The review artifact shows the selected goal, the proposed workspace and the next task together.

Advanced configuration is deferred rather than deleted. Integrations and notification choices would remain available after the first useful action. A later prototype should include the path back to those settings and confirm that participants can find it without being coached.

## What is visible in the work

The comparison contains a five-group baseline and a three-stage proposal. Four artifacts collect the baseline, goal-first screen, review state and component vocabulary. The decision map explains how essential choices, progress and recoverability fit together.

These are counts and relationships in an illustrated concept. They are not evidence of fewer real-world clicks, faster setup, better retention or increased activation. The value of the exercise is a specific proposal that can now be evaluated.

## The next validation

Ask representative participants to describe what they expect after each choice, begin a first task, return to change an earlier choice and locate a deferred setting. Look for hesitation, incorrect expectations and uncertainty about what has been saved.

The most important unresolved question is whether the first offered goals match how people actually describe their intentions. If those categories are wrong, a shorter flow can still lead in the wrong direction. Research should refine the question before polishing the transition between screens.
