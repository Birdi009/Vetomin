## The question

What changes when an onboarding experience starts with a useful goal instead of a setup checklist?

Threshold is an original product-flow concept. It uses constructed example states to explore that question. The research labels describe proposed work and testable hypotheses—not interviews or experiments that have already happened.

## Context and constraint

A product may need an account, workspace, settings, and collaborators eventually. That does not establish that every visitor needs to configure them before understanding what the product can do.

The starting frame asks for six setup actions before a task. The redesigned frame starts with a first goal, demonstrates a guided task, then offers a way to save progress and configure what is needed.

## The design hypothesis

The hypothesis is that a visible outcome can make the setup request more understandable. This is plausible reasoning, not a measured finding. Some products genuinely require configuration first, especially where safety, data integrity, or permissions are involved.

Threshold therefore does not propose removing necessary checks. It distinguishes necessary setup from optional setup and asks each step to explain the value it unlocks.

## The guided task

The example workspace is explicitly labeled. A visitor can inspect a small task without mistaking the sample content for their own data. The interface explains what would be saved and what would remain temporary.

The guided path is intentionally limited. A demonstration should establish a useful mental model, not become a long tutorial the visitor must complete before they are allowed to explore.

## Preserving the choice to leave

Optional steps have visible skip and back paths in the flow proposal. Saving progress is offered at a meaningful moment rather than presented as a condition for understanding the example.

A production implementation would need to explain retention and deletion precisely. This concept does not claim to implement an account system, persistence service, or permission model.

## The rejected direction

A compulsory completion checklist would make setup progress easier to display. It could also reward completion of administrative steps before the visitor has seen a meaningful outcome.

The chosen direction prioritizes the first useful task. The tradeoff is that the example must represent the real product honestly. An unusually polished demo followed by a confusing product would make onboarding less trustworthy, not more.

## Outcome boundary

The two frames demonstrate a change from six setup-first stages to four goal-led stages. They make optional configuration less prominent and give the first task a clearer place in the journey.

No activation, retention, conversion, or completion improvement has been measured. The next step is to test the concept with representative first-time users and inspect whether the promised value matches what they believe they can accomplish.

## The next iteration

Observe the first task without coaching. Ask what the visitor expected, where they hesitated, and why they chose to continue or leave. Then test the return visit: a good first impression should not create a confusing second session.
