## A smaller menu is not the outcome

A workflow redesign can begin with an appealing target: fewer screens, fewer clicks or fewer visible items. Those are convenient things to count. They are not automatically the things a person needs.

Atlas is an original concept study that makes this distinction inspectable. Its constructed starting screen presents seven navigation choices. The alternative presents four groups. That count belongs to the illustrated interface; it is not a measured reduction in task time, errors or effort.

The design question is what each choice means, when it matters and what information needs to stay beside it. Counting is useful only after those relationships are understood.

## Separate actions, objects and configuration

New request is an action. Requests and People are objects or collections someone may return to repeatedly. Files and Reports provide context. Rules and Settings shape how the workspace behaves. Presenting all of them at the same level can make their relationships harder to see in the constructed baseline.

The task inventory names these differences before the new layout is drawn. A grouping map then records where every starting capability goes. This prevents a visual simplification from quietly deleting a requirement.

The classification is still a hypothesis. A reporting specialist may treat Reports as a primary destination, while a request handler may consider it supporting context. The right grouping depends on the real task and role. A concept should disclose that uncertainty rather than hide it behind a polished navigation bar.

## Keep the next decision in context

The Atlas proposal places request information, status and the primary review action in the same workspace. Supporting files remain near their request. Rules move into settings, and a new request becomes an action within the request area.

The important change is not simply a shorter sidebar. It is the relationship between the selected object and what can happen next. Someone should be able to identify what they are acting on before choosing the action.

That relationship also helps frame a test. Instead of asking whether the new menu looks cleaner, ask someone to find a request that needs attention, identify its owner, locate its supporting file and describe the next step. Their route through the task gives the proposal something concrete to answer for.

## Keep a rejected direction in the story

The study includes an all-in-one menu as an alternative that was not selected. It reduces visible navigation even further, but it also makes the available choices less inspectable. The workspace still needs a clear task and its context.

Showing that alternative makes the reasoning less circular. The preferred layout is not presented as good merely because it is the finished one. The reader can see a different direction and the tradeoff that motivated the choice.

This is an account of a design exploration, not a fabricated usability test. No participant was observed rejecting the menu. That distinction matters whenever concept work is presented as evidence of a way of thinking.

## Make the next research question specific

The next evaluation should compare equivalent tasks and content, include the relevant roles and record both completion and misunderstanding. A person can reach the correct destination while holding the wrong idea about what the system did. Ask them to explain the labels and the result, not only to click through them.

Also test the supporting destinations that moved. If Files is now contextual, can someone find a file before remembering which request it belongs to? If Rules lives in Settings, is that reasonable for the people who edit rules frequently?

Designing around decisions does not eliminate screens. It gives the screens a clearer reason to exist. The proposed hierarchy earns its place only when the relationship among task, context and action holds up beyond the illustration.
